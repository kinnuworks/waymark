Why it matters

Inside a building, a blind visitor still has to find someone to ask.

GPS stops at the front door. Indoor guidance today needs beacons on the walls and a paid survey, so almost no library, clinic or college has it.

The usual way

Today: beacons on every wall and a survey team.

  • Bluetooth beacons fixed around the building, each with a battery to replace
  • A vendor visit to survey and calibrate before anyone can use it
  • Often a separate app to install and an account to create
With Waymark

Waymark: two phones and one sheet of paper.

  • A printed A4 ChArUco board by the entrance gives every walk the same origin and real scale
  • Two volunteers walk the floor for about 15 minutes and mark rooms, doors, stairs and lifts
  • Visitors open a free web page, with nothing to install and no account
How it works

Map a floor once. Every blind visitor after that gets spoken directions.

All of the vision runs in OpenCV 5 on one AWS Graviton4 instance. The phones just send camera frames and read out the replies.

Mapping

1. Two volunteers map the floor in 15 minutes.

Both volunteers start at the board, so their walks share one origin and real metric scale from the first frame. The server refines each walk with bundle adjustment, fuses the 3D points they both saw, and draws a floor plan with a route graph.

  • A coordinator splits the unmapped corridors between walkers and tells each one where to go
  • Volunteers tap to mark rooms, doors, stairs, lifts and exits as they pass
  • Routes follow paths people actually walked

Locating

2. The visitor’s phone knows where it is, every second.

About once a second the visitor's phone sends one camera frame and its compass heading. The server finds a shortlist of nearby keyframes, matches features to the map's 3D points, and solves for position and heading.

  • Median 1.77 cm and 1.98° on the 7-Scenes relocalisation protocol
  • 352 ms per frame on one Graviton4 instance
  • Replies come as speech and vibration and work with screen readers. Visitor frames are not stored

Guidance agent

3. When it isn’t sure, it stops the visitor and calls a person.

Every frame, the agent looks at how confident the fix is and decides what to say. Each decision is logged with what it saw and why it acted, and the helper console shows that log live.

  • Asks the visitor to pan when the camera can't see enough, and to slow down when unsure
  • After leaving the route three times, it re-routes with fewer turns
  • Stops before stairs unless it is certain
  • Flags corridors that no longer match the map, and calls a human helper when it can't place the visitor

Architecture

The phone sends a photo. The server sends back one direction.

Phones do no vision work. They send a JPEG about once a second and speak the reply, so any recent phone browser can take part.

Safety

It adds directions. The cane or guide dog still leads.

Waymark says so before every visit. The safety rules live in server code, and a visitor can't talk the agent out of them.

  • It does not see obstacles. The map has walls, doors and stairs. People, trolleys and wet floors are for the cane or dog.
  • It stops before stairs unless the fix is confident, and points the visitor to the handrail.
  • It says when it's unsure and asks the visitor to pan, slow down or wait.
  • A person is one tap away. Call a helper is always on screen, and the agent calls one itself after six frames without a fix.
  • If the connection drops, the phone tells the visitor to stop and wait.
Questions

Does it replace a cane or guide dog? No. Other questions, answered.

How does a blind visitor open it and choose where to go?

Through the screen reader they already use every day: VoiceOver on iPhone or TalkBack on Android. Every control is a labelled button, and the first question asks whether you use a screen reader, so directions are not spoken twice. To open Waymark, the visitor taps a link the venue sends before the visit, or holds the phone to an NFC tag beside the entrance board. Either way, the link opens that building directly (go.html?building=…), with nothing to find or aim at. To choose where to go, they swipe through the list of places, or dictate with the keyboard microphone: "take me to the lift". While walking, Repeat, Where am I? and Call a helper are large buttons.

Does it replace a cane or guide dog?

No. Waymark knows where the walls, doors and stairs are. It knows nothing about people, bags on the floor or a door left open. The visitor keeps their cane or dog, and Waymark tells them which way to go.

What happens when the building changes?

When frames are clear but stop matching the map, the agent flags that area for re-mapping and the helper console shows it. A volunteer then re-walks that corridor. The map does not update itself.

Which phones does it work on?

Any recent iPhone or Android phone with a rear camera and a current browser. All the vision runs on the server, so the phone only sends a photo about once a second. It works with VoiceOver and TalkBack, and without them.

What does it cost to run?

It's free for visitors and buildings. One c8g.xlarge instance costs about $3.83 a day and answers each visitor frame in about a third of a second. The smaller c8g.large costs half that at 0.6 seconds a frame.

What happens to the camera images?

Volunteer walk frames are kept for 30 days so a walk can be replayed, then deleted. Visitor frames are not stored at all. There are no accounts.

How accurate is it?

On the 7-Scenes benchmark, a merged map is within 1.2 cm at the median and visitors are located to 1.77 cm. Real buildings with glass, blank walls or poor light are harder, and the agent says so when they are. Field test numbers come next.

Does it work across floors?

Not yet. Each map covers one floor, and a route ends at a lift or staircase.
